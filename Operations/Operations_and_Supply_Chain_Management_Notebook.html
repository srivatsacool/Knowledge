What is Operations & Supply Chain Management?

Operations Management is the design, execution, and control of the processes that transform inputs (raw materials, capital, labor, data) into finished goods and services. While strategy determines what promise a company makes to the market, operations determines whether that promise can be delivered reliably, at scale, and profitably.

The Operational Reality: "Every business is a collection of processes. If you cannot describe what you are doing as a process, you do not know what you are doing." — W. Edwards Deming

Supply Chain Management (SCM) expands this focus beyond the four walls of the firm to orchestrate the entire end-to-end network of suppliers, manufacturing plants, distribution centers, and retailers that deliver products to the end consumer.

Why Operations Matters in an MBA

Operations is the engine room of enterprise profitability and cash flow. Operating expenses (COGS and SG&A) account for the vast majority of cash outlays in most corporations.

Key operational capabilities required of MBA executives include:

  • Bottleneck Identification: Pinpointing the exact constraint in a workflow that limits total enterprise throughput.
  • Working Capital Release: Slashing excess cycle stock and safety stock across multi-echelon networks to liberate cash.
  • Operational Resilience: Designing supply chains that absorb shocks (pandemics, canal blockages, supplier insolvencies) without halting customer deliveries.

Core Concepts of Process & Supply Chain Design

1. Little’s Law

The fundamental mathematical relationship of queueing and flow: Work-in-Process (WIP) = Throughput Rate (TH) × Flow Time (CT). Holds true for any stable process regardless of arrival distributions.

2. The Theory of Constraints (Goldratt)

Every complex system has at least one constraint (bottleneck) that limits its capacity. An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the entire system; an hour saved at a non-bottleneck is an illusion.

3. Lean Production & Waste Elimination

Originating from the Toyota Production System (TPS). Focuses on eliminating the 7 Wastes (Muda): Overproduction, Waiting, Transport, Inappropriate Processing, Excess Inventory, Motion, and Defects.

4. The Bullwhip Effect

The phenomenon where small fluctuations in end-consumer retail demand amplify into massive, chaotic swings in orders and inventory as they propagate upstream through wholesalers, manufacturers, and raw material suppliers.

SECTION 05 OF 10

Major Frameworks and Analytical Models

1. The 5 Focusing Steps of Theory of Constraints

1. Identify the system's constraint → 2. Exploit the constraint (maximize its output) → 3. Subordinate everything else to the constraint → 4. Elevate the constraint (invest capital to expand capacity) → 5. Repeat (prevent inertia when a new bottleneck emerges).

2. The Product-Process Matrix (Hayes & Wheelwright)

Aligns product volume/variety with process structures: Project (unique, low volume) → Job Shop (high variety) → Batch → Assembly Line (high volume, standardized) → Continuous Flow (commodities, oil/chemicals). Operating off the diagonal destroys cost efficiency.

3. Six Sigma DMAIC Methodology

Data-driven quality improvement framework: Define the problem → Measure current performance → Analyze root causes → Improve process capability → Control process to sustain gains (3.4 defects per million opportunities).

Important Formulas & Quantitative Measures

Little's Law Inventory (I) = Throughput Rate (R) × Flow Time (T)
To reduce flow time without sacrificing throughput, a manager must relentlessly reduce work-in-process (WIP) inventory.
Capacity Utilization Utilization = Actual Output / Maximum Design Capacity
High utilization (> 90%) in high-variability processes causes queue times to explode non-linearly (Kingman's equation).
Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) OEE = (Operating Time / Planned Time) × (Total Units / Theoretical Units) × (Good Units / Total Units)
Multiplies Availability Rate × Performance Efficiency × Quality Yield.

Typical Strategic Business Decisions Involved

Make vs. Buy (Outsourcing)

Deciding whether to manufacture components internally to protect proprietary know-how or outsource to contract manufacturers (e.g., Apple contracting Foxconn) to convert fixed capital into variable costs.

Capacity Timing & Sizing: Lead vs. Lag

Capacity Leading (expanding capacity ahead of demand to capture market share, risking underutilization) vs. Capacity Lagging (expanding only after demand is proven, risking stockouts and lost customers).

Facility Location & Network Design

Optimizing plant and warehouse locations using center-of-gravity methods to minimize freight shipping costs, tariff penalties, and delivery lead times.

Push vs. Pull Supply Chain Strategy

Push: producing to forecast (speculative). Pull: producing to actual customer order (kanban/JIT). Modern supply chains use a push-pull decoupling point (postponement).

SECTION 08 OF 10

Real-World Business Applications

Case 1: Zara’s Fast-Fashion Agile Supply Chain

Unlike competitors who outsource manufacturing 6 to 9 months in advance to Asia, Zara owns flexible European manufacturing plants and uses postponement (dyeing fabrics late). It designs, manufactures, and places new garments in retail stores in just 15 days, virtually eliminating markdown discounts.

Case 2: The COVID-19 Bullwhip Effect in Semiconductor Chips

When automakers canceled microchip orders early in 2020 expecting a recession, semiconductor foundries reallocated capacity to consumer electronics. When auto demand rebounded, automakers faced catastrophic multi-year chip shortages, shutting down entire assembly lines worldwide.

Cross-Disciplinary Connections to Other MBA Fields

  • Finance: Inventory is one of the largest balance sheet current assets; optimizing inventory turnover directly accelerates the Cash Conversion Cycle and boosts ROIC.
  • Marketing: Product availability, fulfillment speed, and on-time delivery (OTIF) are foundational elements of the customer value proposition.
  • Technology & Enterprise Systems: Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), Advanced Planning and Scheduling (APS), and Warehouse Management Systems (WMS) automate the operational execution backbone.
