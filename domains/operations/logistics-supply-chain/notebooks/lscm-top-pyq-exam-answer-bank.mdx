### How to Structure High-Scoring Exam Answers

#### 1. The 5-Mark Question Format (Target: 1.5 – 2 Pages)
1. **Definition & Introduction** (0.5 – 1 Mark): Clear, authoritative opening sentence establishing the core concept.
2. **Process Diagram / Framework** (1 Mark): Clean ASCII or SVG box diagram visualizing the operational relationship.
3. **Structured Key Points** (2 – 2.5 Marks): 4 to 5 crisp, bulleted points explaining mechanics and trade-offs.
4. **Real-World Business Example** (0.5 Mark): Concrete corporate reference (e.g., Maruti Suzuki, Dell, Amazon, Amul).
5. **Managerial Conclusion** (0.5 Mark): One crisp summary sentence synthesizing strategic utility.

#### 2. The 6-Mark Question Format (Target: 2 – 2.5 Pages)
1. **Introduction & Context** (1 Mark): Define both concepts and state the operational problem.
2. **Comparative Matrix / Diagram** (1 Mark): Visual comparison or 2x2 matrix separating the elements.
3. **Detailed Comparison / Key Dimensions** (3 Marks): Minimum 5 distinct operational dimensions analyzed.
4. **Industry Application** (0.5 Mark): Enterprise case study illustration.
5. **Strategic Conclusion** (0.5 Mark): Summary of executive decision criteria.

#### 3. The 10-Mark Master Question Format (Target: 3.5 – 4.5 Pages)
1. **Foundational Definition & Thesis** (1 Mark): Authoritative conceptual framing.
2. **System Architecture / Blueprint** (2 Marks): Full multi-tier diagram or cost trade-off curve.
3. **In-Depth Theoretical Analysis** (4 Marks): Comprehensive exploration of drivers, assumptions, and governance.
4. **Worked Numerical / Enterprise Case Application** (2 Marks): Mathematical trace or detailed case analysis.
5. **Managerial Implication & Conclusion** (1 Mark): Executive summary of risk and decision criteria.

## Q1. Demand & Supply Management Balance

<PYQCard
  year="2025"
  number="1"
  marks={5}
  type="theory"
  topic="Demand & Supply Management Balance"
  frequency="2025 Likely"
  question="Q1. Demand & Supply Management Balance"
  answerSummary="Demand management shapes and forecasts demand; supply management plans capacity, procurement, and logistics. Strategic alignment ensures cost-effective customer service."
>

Explain the Role of Demand Management and Supply Management in an Organisation's Supply Chain.

**Likely PYQ:** 2025
**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

**Demand Management** is the process of capturing, understanding and
influencing market demand so that customer requirements are aligned with
organisational capabilities.

**Supply Management** ensures that the organisation has the required
materials, capacity, suppliers and production resources to satisfy the
expected demand.

## Diagram

``` text
                 SUPPLY CHAIN BALANCE
                        │
          ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
          │                           │
   DEMAND MANAGEMENT            SUPPLY MANAGEMENT
          │                           │
 Forecast demand              Supplier capacity
 Customer signals             Procurement
 Promotions                    Production planning
 Pricing                       Inventory
 Allocations                   Logistics capacity
          │                           │
          └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                        ↓
             CUSTOMER SERVICE + COST
                        ↓
                SUPPLY CHAIN FIT
```

## Key Points

### Demand Management

-   Captures customer demand signals.
-   Uses historical sales, market information and forecasts.
-   Uses pricing, promotions and allocations to shape demand.
-   Converts market requirements into a usable demand plan.
-   Helps avoid both overproduction and stockouts.

### Supply Management

-   Plans procurement of materials.
-   Evaluates supplier capacity and lead time.
-   Balances production capacity with demand.
-   Controls inventory and replenishment.
-   Coordinates transportation and delivery.

### Integration

The objective is not to maximise demand or supply independently. The
objective is to create a **balanced plan** where customer demand can be
served at an acceptable cost and service level.

## Example

During COVID-19, automobile manufacturers initially reduced
semiconductor orders because they expected lower vehicle demand.
Consumer electronics demand increased sharply, so semiconductor capacity
shifted towards electronics. When automobile demand recovered faster
than expected, manufacturers faced severe chip shortages and production
disruptions.

## Conclusion

Demand management determines **what the market is likely to require**,
while supply management determines **how the organisation can fulfil
it**. Their integration is essential for cost, availability and
resilience.

</PYQCard>

## Q2. Efficient vs Responsive Supply Chains (Fisher's Framework)

<PYQCard
  year="2025"
  number="2"
  marks={5}
  type="theory"
  topic="Efficient vs Responsive Supply Chains (Fisher's Framework)"
  frequency="Repeated Core"
  question="Q2. Efficient vs Responsive Supply Chains (Fisher's Framework)"
  answerSummary="Functional products require physically efficient supply chains (low cost, high turns); innovative products require market-responsive supply chains (speed, flexibility)."
>

Compare Efficient and Responsive Supply Chains using Fisher's Framework.

**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

Marshall Fisher argues that supply chain strategy should match the
**demand characteristics of the product**.

-   **Functional products** → predictable demand → efficient supply
    chain.
-   **Innovative products** → uncertain demand → responsive supply
    chain.

## Diagram

``` text
                 PRODUCT DEMAND PROFILE
                         │
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             ↓                       ↓
       FUNCTIONAL                INNOVATIVE
       Predictable               Uncertain
       Low variety               High variety
             │                       │
             ↓                       ↓
        EFFICIENT                RESPONSIVE
             │                       │
       Low cost                 High speed
       High utilisation         Flexibility
       Low inventory            Buffer capacity
       Scale economies          Fast response
```

## Key Points

  Dimension        Efficient SC      Responsive SC
  --- --- ---
  Demand           Predictable       Uncertain
  Main objective   Low cost          Fast response
  Inventory        Low               Strategic buffers
  Capacity         Highly utilised   Flexible
  Production       Stable            Flexible/changeover
  Transport        Cost efficient    Speed oriented

## Example

**Walmart** selling everyday consumer staples can emphasise efficiency,
high utilisation and low logistics cost.

**Zara** operates with a responsive model because fashion demand is
uncertain and product life cycles are short.

## Conclusion

The correct supply chain is not universally the cheapest or fastest. It
is the one that is **strategically aligned with the product's demand
profile**.

</PYQCard>

## Q3. Warehouse Process Flow & Operational Types

<PYQCard
  year="2025"
  number="3"
  marks={5}
  type="theory"
  topic="Warehouse Process Flow & Operational Types"
  frequency="Repeated Core"
  question="Q3. Warehouse Process Flow & Operational Types"
  answerSummary="Warehousing transforms inbound goods into outbound fulfillment via Receiving -> Putaway -> Storage -> Picking -> Packing -> Shipping."
>

Explain the Warehouse Process and Types of Warehouses.

**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

A warehouse is a facility used to receive, store, manage and dispatch
goods while maintaining product availability and service levels.

## Diagram

``` text
RECEIVING
    ↓
PUT-AWAY
    ↓
STORAGE
    ↓
PICKING
    ↓
PACKING
    ↓
SHIPPING
    ↓
RETURNS
```

## Key Points

### Warehouse Process

1.  **Receiving** --- verify quantity, quality and documentation.
2.  **Put-away** --- move goods to assigned storage locations.
3.  **Storage** --- maintain inventory safely.
4.  **Picking** --- retrieve goods against customer orders.
5.  **Packing** --- consolidate and protect goods.
6.  **Shipping** --- dispatch to customers or downstream nodes.
7.  **Returns management** --- inspect and route returned products.

### Major Warehouse Types

-   **Private warehouse:** owned/operated by the company.
-   **Public warehouse:** shared commercial storage facility.
-   **Contract warehouse:** operated under a long-term agreement for a
    specific customer.
-   **Bonded warehouse:** goods are stored under customs control with
    duties generally suspended until applicable release into the
    domestic market.

## Example

An e-commerce fulfilment centre receives inventory, puts it into
storage, picks items against online orders, packs them and dispatches
them through delivery networks.

## Conclusion

Warehousing is not merely storage. It is a **service and flow-management
system** connecting inbound supply with customer fulfilment.

</PYQCard>

## Q5. CPFR & Bullwhip Effect Mitigation

<PYQCard
  year="2025"
  number="5"
  marks={5}
  type="theory"
  topic="CPFR & Bullwhip Effect Mitigation"
  frequency="Repeated Core"
  question="Q5. CPFR & Bullwhip Effect Mitigation"
  answerSummary="Collaborative Planning, Forecasting, and Replenishment synchronizes demand visibility across trading partners to dampen demand distortion."
>

Explain CPFR and its Role in Reducing the Bullwhip Effect.

**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

**CPFR --- Collaborative Planning, Forecasting and Replenishment** ---
is a collaborative approach in which supply-chain partners jointly plan
demand, forecasting and replenishment using shared information.

## Diagram

``` text
        RETAILER / CUSTOMER
                │
          POS / SALES DATA
                ↓
        ┌───────────────┐
        │      CPFR     │
        └───────────────┘
                ↓
          SHARED FORECAST
                ↓
          ORDER FORECAST
                ↓
          MANUFACTURER
                ↓
           SUPPLIER
```

## Key Points

The 9-step framework can be grouped into four stages:

1.  **Strategy & Planning**
    -   Front-end agreement.
    -   Joint business plan.
2.  **Demand & Supply Management**
    -   Sales forecast.
    -   Exception identification.
    -   Exception resolution.
    -   Order forecast.
    -   Order exceptions.
3.  **Execution**
    -   Order generation.
4.  **Analysis**
    -   Order fulfilment and performance assessment.

### Bullwhip Effect

The bullwhip effect occurs when small changes in customer demand create
increasingly large fluctuations as information moves upstream.

CPFR reduces it through: - Shared POS information. - Collaborative
forecasting. - Exception management. - Coordinated promotions. - Common
replenishment plans.

## Example

A retailer sharing actual store-level sales data with a manufacturer
allows the manufacturer to respond to actual consumption rather than
exaggerated distributor orders.

## Conclusion

CPFR improves **information visibility, forecast alignment and
replenishment coordination**, thereby reducing demand distortion and
inventory instability.

</PYQCard>

## Q7. Primary vs Secondary Transportation & Cost Trade-Off

<PYQCard
  year="2024"
  number="7"

  type="theory"

  frequency="Repeated Core"
  question="Q7. Primary vs Secondary Transportation & Cost Trade-Off"
  answerSummary="Primary transport moves bulk shipments from plants to regional hubs (TL, economies of scale); secondary transport delivers consolidated small lots to retail points (LTL, higher cost per unit)."
>

Explain Primary and Secondary Transportation and the Logistics Cost Trade-Off.

**Marks:** 6

## Introduction

Transportation connects supply-chain nodes and determines the speed,
cost and reliability with which goods reach customers.

## Diagram

``` text
SUPPLIER / FACTORY
       │
       │ PRIMARY TRANSPORT
       │ Long-haul / FTL / FCL
       ↓
 REGIONAL / NATIONAL DC
       │
       │ SECONDARY TRANSPORT
       │ LTL / Milk-run / Last-mile
       ↓
RETAILER / CUSTOMER
```

## Key Points

### Primary Transportation

-   Factory to DC / regional hub.
-   Generally long-distance.
-   Larger loads.
-   FTL/FCL commonly used.
-   Focus: cost per ton-km and vehicle/container utilisation.
-   Consolidation and backhauls improve efficiency.

### Secondary Transportation

-   DC to stores/customers.
-   Smaller and fragmented shipments.
-   LTL, milk-runs and last-mile delivery.
-   Higher delivery frequency.
-   Focus: cost per drop, route efficiency and service level.

### Cost-Service Trade-Off

``` text
FAST / RESPONSIVE
Air / Road
High cost
Short lead time
       ↕
EFFICIENT
Rail / Ocean
Low unit cost
Longer lead time
```

## Example

An FMCG company can use large truckloads from a plant to a regional DC
and smaller milk-runs from the DC to multiple retailers.

## Conclusion

Primary transport optimises **line-haul efficiency**, while secondary
transport optimises **distribution density and customer
responsiveness**.

</PYQCard>

## Q8. Logistics Network Design & Centralisation vs Decentralisation

<PYQCard
  year="2024"
  number="8"
  marks={6}
  type="theory"
  topic="Logistics Network Design & Centralisation vs Decentralisation"
  frequency="2024, 2025"
  question="Q8. Logistics Network Design & Centralisation vs Decentralisation"
  answerSummary="Centralization cuts facility and safety stock costs via risk pooling but increases outbound freight; decentralization improves local delivery speed but inflates inventory."
>

Explain Logistics Network Design and the Centralisation--Decentralisation Trade-Off.

**Marks:** 6

## Introduction

Logistics network design determines the number, location and role of
facilities, transportation links and inventory positions required to
serve customers at an acceptable total cost.

## Diagram

``` text
                  FACTORY
                     │
          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
          ↓                     ↓
       CENTRAL DC           REGIONAL DCs
          │                     │
          ↓                 ┌───┴───┐
      Customers             ↓       ↓
                         Stores   Customers
```

## Key Points

### Centralised Network

**Advantages** - Lower facility cost. - Inventory pooling. - Economies
of scale. - Easier control.

**Disadvantages** - Longer customer distance. - Higher outbound
distance. - Potentially slower response.

### Decentralised Network

**Advantages** - Customer proximity. - Faster delivery. - Better local
responsiveness.

**Disadvantages** - More facilities. - Higher facility cost. - Inventory
duplication. - More complex coordination.

### Core Trade-Off

``` text
More Facilities
      ↓
Customer distance ↓
Service level ↑
Facility cost ↑
Inventory ↑
Complexity ↑
```

## Example

An e-commerce firm may centralise slow-moving inventory nationally while
placing fast-moving SKUs in regional fulfilment centres.

## Conclusion

The optimum network balances **total logistics cost, service level,
inventory risk, flexibility and future growth**.

</PYQCard>

## Q9. The Kraljic Purchasing Portfolio Matrix

<PYQCard
  year="2024"
  number="9"

  type="theory"

  frequency="2023, 2024"
  question="Q9. The Kraljic Purchasing Portfolio Matrix"
  answerSummary="Categorizes procurement into Routine, Leverage, Bottleneck, and Strategic quadrants based on Profit Impact and Supply Risk."
>

Explain the Kraljic Purchasing Portfolio Matrix.

**Marks:** 6

## Introduction

The Kraljic Matrix classifies purchased items according to **profit
impact** and **supply risk**, allowing firms to choose appropriate
sourcing strategies.

## Diagram

``` text
                 SUPPLY RISK
                    HIGH
                     │
       BOTTLENECK    │    STRATEGIC
                     │
                     │
LOW PROFIT ──────────┼────────── HIGH PROFIT
IMPACT               │              IMPACT
                     │
        ROUTINE      │    LEVERAGE
                     │
                    LOW
```

## Key Points

### 1. Routine / Non-Critical

-   Low profit impact.
-   Low supply risk.
-   Standardised items.
-   Strategy: automate, consolidate suppliers, e-procurement.

### 2. Leverage

-   High profit impact.
-   Low supply risk.
-   Multiple suppliers.
-   Strategy: exploit volume, negotiate aggressively, competitive
    bidding.

### 3. Bottleneck

-   Low profit impact.
-   High supply risk.
-   Scarce/proprietary supply.
-   Strategy: secure continuity, buffers, alternate specifications.

### 4. Strategic

-   High profit impact.
-   High supply risk.
-   Critical components.
-   Strategy: long-term partnerships, joint development, risk sharing
    and supplier collaboration.

## Example

A semiconductor critical to an automobile's ECU can be a **strategic
item**, while standard office stationery is generally **routine**.

## Conclusion

Kraljic prevents a company from using the same purchasing strategy for
every item.

</PYQCard>

## Q11. The SCOR Framework & Supply Chain Performance Metrics

<PYQCard
  year="2024"
  number="11"
  marks={6}
  type="theory"
  topic="The SCOR Framework & Supply Chain Performance Metrics"
  frequency="2023 + Syllabus"
  question="Q11. The SCOR Framework & Supply Chain Performance Metrics"
  answerSummary="Standardizes cross-industry processes into Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return, Enable across Reliability, Responsiveness, Agility, Cost, and Asset Management."
>

Explain the SCOR Framework and Important Supply Chain Metrics.

**Marks:** 6

## Introduction

SCOR is a standard framework used to describe, measure and evaluate
supply-chain processes and performance.

## Diagram

``` text
          ┌────── PLAN ──────┐
          │                  │
          ↓                  ↓
       SOURCE → MAKE → DELIVER
          ↑                  │
          └──── RETURN ←─────┘

       + ORCHESTRATE / ENABLE
```

## Key Points

### Major Process Areas

1.  **Plan** --- balance demand and supply.
2.  **Source** --- procure goods/services.
3.  **Make** --- transform inputs into outputs.
4.  **Deliver** --- fulfil and distribute orders.
5.  **Return** --- manage reverse flows.
6.  Modern SCOR versions also emphasise orchestration/enabling
    capabilities.

### Important Metrics

-   **Inventory Turnover** = COGS / Average Inventory.
-   **Order Fill Rate** = Fully shipped orders / Total orders × 100.
-   **On-Time Delivery %**.
-   **Perfect Order Rate**.

Explain EOQ in Detail and Solve a Numerical Problem.

**Marks:** 10
**PYQ priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

## Introduction

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) determines the order quantity that
minimises the combined **ordering cost and inventory holding cost**,
assuming relatively stable demand and other classical EOQ assumptions.

## Diagram

``` text
ANNUAL COST
   │
   │\        Holding Cost
   │ \          /
   │  \        /
   │   \      /
   │    \    /
   │     \  /
   │      \/  ← Minimum Total Cost
   │      /
   │     /  
   │    /    
   │   /      
   └────────────────────→ ORDER QUANTITY
             Q*
```

## Formula

$$
EOQ = Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}
$$

Where: - (D) = annual demand - (S) = ordering/setup cost per order - (H)
= annual holding cost per unit

Total relevant annual cost:

$$
TC = \frac{D}{Q}S + \frac{Q}{2}H
$$

## Key Points

At EOQ: - Ordering cost decreases as order quantity increases. - Holding
cost increases as order quantity increases. - EOQ is the point at which
total relevant cost is minimum. - Average cycle inventory = (Q/2),
assuming no safety stock. - At the mathematical EOQ, annual ordering
cost approximately equals annual holding cost.

### Classical Assumptions

-   Demand is known and relatively constant.
-   Lead time is known.
-   Replenishment is instantaneous.
-   No stockouts.
-   No quantity discounts.
-   Holding and ordering costs are known.

## Numerical Problem

**Given:** - Annual demand (D = 12,000) units - Ordering cost (S = \₹50)
per order - Holding cost (H = \₹4) per unit/year - Lead time (L = 5)
working days - Working days = 300/year

### Step 1 --- Calculate EOQ

$$
Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}
$$

$$
= \sqrt{\frac{2(12,000)(50)}{4}}
$$

$$
= \sqrt{300,000}
$$

$$
Q^* = 547.72
$$

$$
\boxed{EOQ \approx 548\ units}
$$

### Step 2 --- Orders per Year

$$
N = \frac{D}{Q}
$$

$$
N = \frac{12,000}{548}
$$

$$
N \approx 21.90
$$

$$
\boxed{N \approx 22\ orders/year}
$$

### Step 3 --- Annual Ordering Cost

$$
OC = \frac{D}{Q}S
$$

$$
= \frac{12,000}{548}(50)
$$

$$
\approx \₹1,094.89
$$

### Step 4 --- Annual Holding Cost

$$
HC = \frac{Q}{2}H
$$

$$
= \frac{548}{2}(4)
$$

$$
= \₹1,096
$$

### Step 5 --- Total Relevant Annual Cost

$$
TC = OC + HC
$$

$$
TC = 1,094.89 + 1,096
$$

$$
\boxed{TC \approx \₹2,190.89/year}
$$

### Step 6 --- Cycle Time

$$
T = \frac{Q}{D}\times300
$$

$$
= \frac{548}{12,000}\times300
$$

$$
\boxed{T \approx 13.7\ working\ days}
$$

### Step 7 --- Reorder Point

Daily demand:

$$
d = \frac{12,000}{300}=40 units/day
$$

Deterministic ROP:

$$
ROP=dL
$$

$$
=40(5)
$$

$$
\boxed{ROP=200\ units}
$$

## Interpretation

The firm should order approximately **548 units whenever inventory
reaches 200 units**. The 200-unit ROP covers expected demand during the

Explain Safety Stock and Reorder Point under Demand and Lead-Time Uncertainty.

**Marks:** 10
**PYQ priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

## Introduction

Safety stock is additional inventory maintained to protect against
uncertainty in **demand and/or replenishment lead time**. Reorder Point
(ROP) determines when a replenishment order should be placed.

## Diagram

``` text
INVENTORY
   │
   │
   │ 
SS │  \      ← Safety Stock
   │   
   │    
ROP│-----\--- Order Trigger
   │      
   │       
   │        
   │         
   └────────────────────────→ TIME
            ↑
       Replenishment arrives
```

## Core Formulas

### Demand variable, lead time constant

$$
SS=z\sigma_d\sqrt{L}
$$

### Lead time variable, demand constant

$$
SS=z d\sigma_L
$$

### Both demand and lead time variable

$$
SS=z\sqrt{L\sigma_d^2+d^2\sigma_L^2}
$$

### Reorder Point

$$
ROP=dL+SS
$$

Where: - (z) = service-level factor - (d) = average daily demand - (L) =
average lead time - (\sigma_d) = standard deviation of daily
demand - (\sigma_L) = standard deviation of lead time

## Numerical Problem

A distributor has: - Mean daily demand (d=120) units - Daily demand SD
(\sigma_d=30) - Mean lead time (L=16) days - Lead-time SD
(\sigma_L=3) days - Target service level = 95% - (z=1.645)

### Step 1 --- Expected Lead-Time Demand

$$
DL=dL
$$

$$
=120(16)
$$

$$
\boxed{DL=1,920\ units}
$$

### Step 2 --- Combined Standard Deviation

$$
\sigma_{DL} = \sqrt{L\sigma_d^2+d^2\sigma_L^2}
$$

$$
\sqrt{16(30^2)+120^2(3^2)}
$$

$$
\sqrt{16(900)+14,400(9)}
$$

$$
\sqrt{14,400+129,600}
$$

$$
\sqrt{144,000}
$$

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{DL}\approx379.47}
$$

### Step 3 --- Safety Stock

$$
SS=z\sigma_{DL}
$$

$$
=1.645(379.47)
$$

$$
=624.23
$$

$$
\boxed{SS\approx625\ units}
$$

### Step 4 --- Reorder Point

$$
ROP=DL+SS
$$

$$
=1,920+625
$$

$$
\boxed{ROP=2,545\ units}
$$

## Interpretation

When inventory position falls to approximately **2,545 units**, the firm
should trigger replenishment. The 625 units above expected lead-time
demand provide protection against uncertainty.

## Example

An electronics distributor facing volatile demand and supplier lead
times can increase safety stock when the required service level
increases.

## Conclusion

Safety stock converts uncertainty into a measurable inventory buffer,

## Q14. Single-Period / Newsvendor Marginal Analysis

<PYQCard

Explain the Single-Period / Newsvendor Model and Solve a Numerical.

**Marks:** 10
**PYQ priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

## Introduction

The **single-period or Newsvendor model** is used when inventory has a
limited selling period and the decision-maker must balance the cost of
ordering too little against ordering too much.

Typical examples include: - Fashion products. - Fresh food. - Seasonal
goods. - Newspapers. - Event merchandise.

## Diagram

``` text
             SINGLE-PERIOD DECISION
                       │
          ┌────────────┴────────────┐
          ↓                         ↓
     ORDER TOO LITTLE          ORDER TOO MUCH
          │                         │
     UNDERSTOCK                 OVERSTOCK
          │                         │
   Lost contribution          Unsold inventory
       margin                  Salvage loss
          │                         │
          └────────────┬────────────┘
                       ↓
               OPTIMAL QUANTITY
```

## Formula

Understocking cost:

$$
C_u=P-C
$$

Overstocking cost:

$$
C_o=C-S
$$

Critical fractile:

$$
CR=\frac{C_u}{C_u+C_o}
$$

For normally distributed demand:

$$
Q^*=\mu+z\sigma
$$

where:

$$
\Phi(z)=CR
$$

## Numerical Problem

Given: - Selling price (P=\₹400) - Purchase cost (C=\₹150) - Salvage
value (S=\₹50) - Mean demand (\mu=1,200) - Demand SD
(\sigma=250)

### Step 1 --- Understock Cost

$$
C_u=P-C
$$

$$
=400-150
$$

$$
\boxed{C_u=\₹250}
$$

### Step 2 --- Overstock Cost

$$
C_o=C-S
$$

$$
=150-50
$$

$$
\boxed{C_o=\₹100}
$$

### Step 3 --- Critical Fractile

$$
CR=\frac{250}{250+100}
$$

$$
=\frac{250}{350}
$$

$$
\boxed{CR=0.7143}
$$

Thus the target service probability is **71.43%**.

### Step 4 --- Find (z)

From the standard normal table:

$$
\Phi(z)=0.7143
$$

$$
z\approx0.566
$$

### Step 5 --- Calculate Optimal Quantity

$$
Q^*=\mu+z\sigma
$$

$$
=1,200+(0.566)(250)
$$

$$
=1,200+141.5
$$

$$
\boxed{Q^*\approx1,342\ coats}
$$

### Step 6 --- Stockout Probability

$$
P(\text{stockout})=1-CR
$$

$$
=1-0.7143
$$

$$
\boxed{P(\text{stockout})=28.57\%}
$$

## Interpretation

Because the profit lost from understocking is much larger than the loss
from overstocking, the firm should stock more than the mean demand.

## Example

A fashion retailer selling a winter collection cannot keep the same
inventory decision after the season because the remaining products may
need to be heavily discounted.

## Conclusion

The Newsvendor model gives the **economically optimal service level and
stocking quantity** when demand is uncertain and the selling period is
limited.

**Incoterms 2020**, published by the International Chamber of Commerce

``` text
  │

  │                 ↓
  │                DDP
  │

```

## Two Main Groups

### A. Any Mode of Transport

-   EXW
-   FCA
-   CPT
-   CIP
-   DAP
-   DPU
-   DDP

### B. Sea / Inland Waterway Only

-   FAS
-   FOB
-   CFR
-   CIF

## Key Concept: Cost ≠ Risk

This is a major examination trap.

Under **C rules** such as CPT, CIP, CFR and CIF, the seller may pay the
main carriage, but **risk can transfer much earlier**.

## Comparison

  Term                    Main Meaning            Risk Transfer
  --- --- ---
  EXW                     Seller makes goods      Seller's premises
                          available               

  FCA                     Seller delivers to      Named delivery point
                          carrier/named point     

  CPT                     Seller pays carriage    When goods handed to
                                                  carrier

  CIP                     CPT + seller-arranged   When goods handed to

  DAP                     Delivered at            Destination before
                          destination, ready for  unloading

                          duties paid             import clearance

                                                  vessel

  CFR                     Cost and Freight        When goods are on board
                                                  vessel

  CIF                     Cost, Insurance and     When goods are on board
                          Freight                 vessel

## FOB vs CIF

### FOB

-   Sea/inland waterway.
-   Seller loads goods on board.
-   Buyer normally arranges main ocean freight and insurance.
-   Risk transfers once goods are on board.

### CIF

-   Sea/inland waterway.
-   Seller pays ocean freight to destination port.
-   Seller arranges the required insurance.
-   **Risk still transfers when goods are on board at origin.**

Therefore:

> **CIF does not mean seller bears transit risk until destination.**

## FCA and Containers

For containerised and multimodal shipments, **FCA is generally more
appropriate than FOB**, depending on the contractual arrangement,
because FOB is designed for sea/inland-waterway delivery where goods are
loaded directly on board the vessel.

## DDP

DDP places the greatest overall obligation on the seller because the
seller is responsible for delivery with import clearance and applicable
import duties/taxes under the term.

## Example

An Indian exporter shipping a container through a multimodal transport
chain may prefer FCA rather than FOB. A bulk commodity loaded directly
onto a vessel may be appropriately contracted under FOB.

## Conclusion

The key to answering Incoterms questions is to distinguish:

**Who pays? → Who performs? → Where does risk transfer? → What transport
mode applies?**

Explain Supply Chain Resilience and the Impact of Global Disruptions.

**Marks:** 10

## Introduction

Supply chain resilience is the ability of a supply network to **prepare
for, absorb, respond to and recover from disruptions** while maintaining
critical operations.

## Diagram

``` text
               DISRUPTION
                    ↓
        ┌───────────┴───────────┐
        ↓                       ↓
   ABSORB IMPACT            RESPOND
        │                       │
 Buffer inventory          Alternate route
 Backup capacity           Alternate supplier
        │                       │
        └───────────┬───────────┘
                    ↓
                 RECOVER
                    ↓
                 LEARN
                    ↓
             MORE RESILIENT SC
```

## Major Sources of Risk

-   Geopolitical conflict.
-   Port closures.
-   Natural disasters.
-   Pandemics.
-   Supplier bankruptcy.
-   Cyberattacks.
-   Trade restrictions.
-   Transportation disruptions.
-   Single-source dependency.

## Resilience Strategies

### 1. Dual Sourcing

Avoid excessive dependence on one supplier.

### 2. Geographic Diversification

Spread suppliers and facilities across locations.

### 3. Strategic Inventory

Hold buffers for critical/high-risk components.

### 4. Flexible Capacity

Maintain alternate production capability.

### 5. Route Diversification

Use alternate transportation corridors.

### 6. Visibility

Use real-time supply-chain information and control towers.

### 7. Scenario Planning

Simulate disruptions before they occur.

### 8. Supplier Risk Monitoring

Monitor financial health, geopolitical exposure, capacity, ESG and
quality.

## Example

A disruption around a major maritime corridor can force vessels to take
longer routes, increasing lead times and consuming additional capacity.
Firms can respond through alternative routes, supplier diversification
and targeted inventory buffers.

## Conclusion

Resilience does not mean eliminating every disruption. It means
designing a supply chain that can **survive, respond and recover without
unacceptable business impact**.

CASE STUDY ANSWER FRAMEWORK

## Universal 10-Mark Case Structure

When the paper gives a supply-chain case, use this structure.

### 1. Define the Problem

State the core issue in one sentence.

> "The central problem is not merely high logistics cost; it is the
> trade-off between service level, cost and network structure."

### 2. Identify Root Causes

``` text
SYMPTOM
   ↓
CAUSE
   ↓
ROOT CAUSE
```

### 3. Identify Constraints

Examples: - Budget. - Capacity. - Lead time. - Supplier dependency. -
Customer service target. - Regulatory constraints.

### 4. Quantify

Calculate wherever possible: - EOQ. - Safety stock. - ROP. - Total
logistics cost. - Inventory turnover. - Service level. - Landed cost.

### 5. Generate Alternatives

  Option   Cost     Service   Risk
  -------- -------- --------- --------
  A        Low      Low       High
  B        Medium   Medium    Medium
  C        High     High      Low

### 6. Recommend

Use a clear statement:

> **"I recommend Option B because it provides the best
> cost-service-resilience balance."**

### 7. Implementation

``` text
Pilot
 ↓
Measure KPI
 ↓
Correct
 ↓
Scale
 ↓
Monitor
```

### 8. KPIs

Use relevant metrics: - OTIF. - Fill rate. - Inventory turnover. - Cost
per shipment. - Forecast accuracy. - Warehouse utilisation. - Order
cycle time.

### 9. Risks

Mention at least two.

### 10. Conclusion

End with the managerial implication.

Explain Reverse Logistics.

**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

Reverse logistics manages the movement of goods from the customer or
downstream point back toward the producer or another recovery point.

## Diagram

``` text
CUSTOMER
   ↓
RETURN
   ↓
COLLECTION
   ↓
INSPECTION / GRADING
   ↓
┌────┬──────────┬─────────┬─────────┐
↓    ↓          ↓         ↓
RESTOCK REPAIR  RESELL  RECYCLE
```

## Key Points

-   Handles product returns.
-   Supports repair and refurbishment.
-   Recovers value from returned goods.
-   Reduces waste.
-   Supports circular supply chains.
-   Improves customer service.

### 5R Logic

**Return → Repair → Remanufacture → Resell → Recycle**

## Example

An electronics retailer accepts a returned device, tests it, refurbishes
usable units and sends defective components for recycling.

## Conclusion

Reverse logistics converts the **backward flow of products into an
opportunity for value recovery and sustainability**.

## Q23. The Total Logistics Cost Concept & Trade-Off Balance

<PYQCard
  year="2024"
  number="23"
  marks={6}
  type="theory"
  topic="The Total Logistics Cost Concept & Trade-Off Balance"
  frequency="Repeated Core"
  question="Q23. The Total Logistics Cost Concept & Trade-Off Balance"
  answerSummary="Optimizing logistics requires minimizing the sum of Transport + Warehousing + Inventory Carrying + Order Processing + Lost Sales costs simultaneously."
>

Explain the Total Logistics Cost Concept.

**Marks:** 5

## Introduction

Total logistics cost is the combined cost of all activities required to
move and hold products through the supply chain.

## Diagram

``` text
             TOTAL LOGISTICS COST
                     │
 ┌────────┬──────────┼──────────┬─────────┐
 ↓        ↓          ↓          ↓         ↓
Transport Warehousing Inventory Order   Packaging
                                Processing
```

## Components

-   Transportation.
-   Warehousing.
-   Inventory carrying cost.
-   Order processing.
-   Packaging.
-   Loss/damage.
-   Related handling costs.

### Formula

$$
TLC=Transport+Warehousing+Inventory+Order Processing+Packaging+\cdots
$$

### Logistics Cost % of Sales

$$
=\frac{Total\ Logistics\ Cost}{Gross\ Revenue}\times100
$$

## Example

A firm should not select a transport option only because its freight
charge is low if it creates higher inventory, warehousing or stockout
costs.

## Conclusion

Logistics decisions should minimise **total system cost**, not an
isolated cost component.

</PYQCard>

## Q24. SCOR / Supply Chain Drivers & Strategic Fit

<PYQCard

Explain SCOR / Supply Chain Drivers and Strategic Fit.

**Marks:** 6

## Introduction

A supply chain's performance depends on how its structural and
operational drivers are configured to support the firm's competitive
strategy.

## Six Drivers

``` text
             SUPPLY CHAIN DRIVERS
                    │
 ┌──────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┐
 ↓      ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓
Facilities Inventory Transport Information Sourcing Pricing
```

## Key Points

### Facilities

Location, capacity and centralisation.

### Inventory

Cycle stock, safety stock and working capital.

### Transportation

Mode, route, speed and consolidation.

### Information

Visibility, forecasting and coordination.

### Sourcing

Supplier selection, outsourcing and partnerships.

### Pricing

Pricing decisions influence demand patterns and order behaviour.

## Strategic Fit

``` text
Low uncertainty + predictable demand
             ↓
        EFFICIENCY

High uncertainty + innovative products
             ↓
        RESPONSIVENESS
```

## Example

A commodity FMCG product can prioritise scale and low logistics cost,
while a short-life fashion product may prioritise flexibility and speed.

## Conclusion

Supply-chain drivers should be configured as an integrated system rather
than optimised independently.

# 09 — APPENDIX: Faculty Provenance, Cross-Topic Nomenclature & Final Exam Strategy

### Final Examination Hall Strategy & Scoring Blueprint

## For a 5-Mark Question

**Definition → Diagram → 4 key points → Example → Conclusion**

## For a 6-Mark Question

**Definition → Diagram/Matrix → Detailed comparison → Example →
Conclusion**

## For a 10-Mark Question

**Definition → Large diagram → Detailed framework →
Formula/application/case → Managerial interpretation → Conclusion**

## For a Numerical

Always write:

``` text
GIVEN
  ↓
FORMULA
  ↓
SUBSTITUTION
  ↓
CALCULATION
  ↓
FINAL ANSWER
  ↓
INTERPRETATION
```

### Golden Rule

> **Do not stop at the numerical answer. Explain what the number means
> operationally.**

For example:

> "Therefore, the EOQ is 548 units. The firm should order approximately
> 548 units per cycle; when inventory position reaches the ROP of 200
> units, replenishment should be triggered."

That final sentence converts a **calculation into a management answer**.

## One-Line Memory Hooks

-   **EOQ:** Balance ordering cost and holding cost.
-   **ROP:** When to order.
-   **Safety Stock:** Protection against uncertainty.
-   **Newsvendor:** How much to stock for one selling period.
-   **S&OP:** One agreed demand-supply plan.
-   **S&OE:** Short-term execution control.
-   **CPFR:** Collaborate using shared information.
-   **Kraljic:** Match sourcing strategy to profit impact and supply
    risk.
-   **SCOR:** Plan → Source → Make → Deliver → Return.
-   **Incoterms:** Know mode + cost + risk transfer.
-   **Smart Warehouse:** Sense → Decide → Act → Learn.
-   **Network Design:** Balance cost, service, inventory and resilience.
-   **ESG:** Environmental + Social + Governance.
-   **Reverse Logistics:** Move products/value backward.
-   **Resilience:** Prepare → Absorb → Respond → Recover → Learn.
