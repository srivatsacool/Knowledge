Q1. Explain the Role of Demand Management and Supply Management in an Organisation's Supply Chain.

5 Marks 2025 Likely Paper

Likely PYQ: 2025 Marks: 5

Introduction

Demand Management is the process of capturing, understanding and influencing market demand so that customer requirements are aligned with organisational capabilities.

Supply Management ensures that the organisation has the required materials, capacity, suppliers and production resources to satisfy the expected demand.

Diagram

                 SUPPLY CHAIN BALANCE
                        │
          ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
          │                           │
   DEMAND MANAGEMENT            SUPPLY MANAGEMENT
          │                           │
 Forecast demand              Supplier capacity
 Customer signals             Procurement
 Promotions                    Production planning
 Pricing                       Inventory
 Allocations                   Logistics capacity
          │                           │
          └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                        ↓
             CUSTOMER SERVICE + COST
                        ↓
                SUPPLY CHAIN FIT

Key Points

Demand Management

  • Captures customer demand signals.
  • Uses historical sales, market information and forecasts.
  • Uses pricing, promotions and allocations to shape demand.
  • Converts market requirements into a usable demand plan.
  • Helps avoid both overproduction and stockouts.

Supply Management

  • Plans procurement of materials.
  • Evaluates supplier capacity and lead time.
  • Balances production capacity with demand.
  • Controls inventory and replenishment.
  • Coordinates transportation and delivery.

Integration

The objective is not to maximise demand or supply independently. The objective is to create a balanced plan where customer demand can be served at an acceptable cost and service level.

Example

During COVID-19, automobile manufacturers initially reduced semiconductor orders because they expected lower vehicle demand. Consumer electronics demand increased sharply, so semiconductor capacity shifted towards electronics. When automobile demand recovered faster than expected, manufacturers faced severe chip shortages and production disruptions.

Conclusion

Demand management determines what the market is likely to require, while supply management determines how the organisation can fulfil it. Their integration is essential for cost, availability and resilience.

Q2. Compare Efficient and Responsive Supply Chains using Fisher's Framework.

5 Marks 2025 Likely Paper

Marks: 5

Introduction

Marshall Fisher argues that supply chain strategy should match the demand characteristics of the product.

  • Functional products → predictable demand → efficient supply chain.
  • Innovative products → uncertain demand → responsive supply chain.

Diagram

                 PRODUCT DEMAND PROFILE
                         │
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             ↓                       ↓
       FUNCTIONAL                INNOVATIVE
       Predictable               Uncertain
       Low variety               High variety
             │                       │
             ↓                       ↓
        EFFICIENT                RESPONSIVE
             │                       │
       Low cost                 High speed
       High utilisation         Flexibility
       Low inventory            Buffer capacity
       Scale economies          Fast response

Key Points

Dimension Efficient SC Responsive SC

Demand Predictable Uncertain Main objective Low cost Fast response Inventory Low Strategic buffers Capacity Highly utilised Flexible Production Stable Flexible/changeover Transport Cost efficient Speed oriented

Example

Walmart selling everyday consumer staples can emphasise efficiency, high utilisation and low logistics cost.

Zara operates with a responsive model because fashion demand is uncertain and product life cycles are short.

Conclusion

The correct supply chain is not universally the cheapest or fastest. It is the one that is strategically aligned with the product's demand profile.

Q3. Explain the Warehouse Process and Types of Warehouses.

5 Marks 2025 Likely Paper

Marks: 5

Introduction

A warehouse is a facility used to receive, store, manage and dispatch goods while maintaining product availability and service levels.

Diagram

RECEIVING
    ↓
PUT-AWAY
    ↓
STORAGE
    ↓
PICKING
    ↓
PACKING
    ↓
SHIPPING
    ↓
RETURNS

Key Points

Warehouse Process

  1. Receiving --- verify quantity, quality and documentation.
  2. Put-away --- move goods to assigned storage locations.
  3. Storage --- maintain inventory safely.
  4. Picking --- retrieve goods against customer orders.
  5. Packing --- consolidate and protect goods.
  6. Shipping --- dispatch to customers or downstream nodes.
  7. Returns management --- inspect and route returned products.

Major Warehouse Types

  • Private warehouse: owned/operated by the company.
  • Public warehouse: shared commercial storage facility.
  • Contract warehouse: operated under a long-term agreement for a specific customer.
  • Bonded warehouse: goods are stored under customs control with duties generally suspended until applicable release into the domestic market.

Example

An e-commerce fulfilment centre receives inventory, puts it into storage, picks items against online orders, packs them and dispatches them through delivery networks.

Conclusion

Warehousing is not merely storage. It is a service and flow-management system connecting inbound supply with customer fulfilment.

Q5. Explain CPFR and its Role in Reducing the Bullwhip Effect.

5 Marks 2025 Likely Paper

Marks: 5

Introduction

CPFR --- Collaborative Planning, Forecasting and Replenishment --- is a collaborative approach in which supply-chain partners jointly plan demand, forecasting and replenishment using shared information.

Diagram

        RETAILER / CUSTOMER
                │
          POS / SALES DATA
                ↓
        ┌───────────────┐
        │      CPFR     │
        └───────────────┘
                ↓
          SHARED FORECAST
                ↓
          ORDER FORECAST
                ↓
          MANUFACTURER
                ↓
           SUPPLIER

Key Points

The 9-step framework can be grouped into four stages:

  1. Strategy & Planning
    • Front-end agreement.
    • Joint business plan.
  2. Demand & Supply Management
    • Sales forecast.
    • Exception identification.
    • Exception resolution.
    • Order forecast.
    • Order exceptions.
  3. Execution
    • Order generation.
  4. Analysis
    • Order fulfilment and performance assessment.

Bullwhip Effect

The bullwhip effect occurs when small changes in customer demand create increasingly large fluctuations as information moves upstream.

CPFR reduces it through: - Shared POS information. - Collaborative forecasting. - Exception management. - Coordinated promotions. - Common replenishment plans.

Example

A retailer sharing actual store-level sales data with a manufacturer allows the manufacturer to respond to actual consumption rather than exaggerated distributor orders.

Conclusion

CPFR improves information visibility, forecast alignment and replenishment coordination, thereby reducing demand distortion and inventory instability.

6 Marks 2024 Core Paper

Marks: 6

Introduction

Transportation connects supply-chain nodes and determines the speed, cost and reliability with which goods reach customers.

Primary Transportation

  • Factory to DC / regional hub.

An FMCG company can use large truckloads from a plant to a regional DC and smaller milk-runs from the DC to multiple retailers.

Q8. Explain Logistics Network Design and the Centralisation--Decentralisation Trade-Off.

6 Marks 2024 Core Paper

Marks: 6

Introduction

Logistics network design determines the number, location and role of facilities, transportation links and inventory positions required to serve customers at an acceptable total cost.

Diagram

                  FACTORY
                     │
          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
          ↓                     ↓
       CENTRAL DC           REGIONAL DCs
          │                     │
          ↓                 ┌───┴───┐

Key Points

Centralised Network

Advantages - Lower facility cost. - Inventory pooling. - Economies of scale. - Easier control.

Disadvantages - Longer customer distance. - Higher outbound distance. - Potentially slower response.

Decentralised Network

Advantages - Customer proximity. - Faster delivery. - Better local responsiveness.

Disadvantages - More facilities. - Higher facility cost. - Inventory duplication. - More complex coordination.

Core Trade-Off

More Facilities
      ↓

Service level ↑
Facility cost ↑
Inventory ↑
Complexity ↑

Example

An e-commerce firm may centralise slow-moving inventory nationally while placing fast-moving SKUs in regional fulfilment centres.

Conclusion

The optimum network balances total logistics cost, service level, inventory risk, flexibility and future growth.

The Kraljic Matrix classifies purchased items according to profit impact and supply risk, allowing firms to choose appropriate sourcing strategies.

                 SUPPLY RISK

Key Points

1. Routine / Non-Critical

  • Low profit impact.
  • Low supply risk.
  • Standardised items.
  • Strategy: automate, consolidate suppliers, e-procurement.

2. Leverage

  • High profit impact.
  • Low supply risk.
  • Multiple suppliers.
  • Strategy: exploit volume, negotiate aggressively, competitive bidding.

  • Low profit impact.
  • High supply risk.
  • Scarce/proprietary supply.

  • High profit impact.
  • High supply risk.
  • Critical components.
  • Strategy: long-term partnerships, joint development, risk sharing and supplier collaboration.

Example

A semiconductor critical to an automobile's ECU can be a strategic item, while standard office stationery is generally routine.

Conclusion

Kraljic prevents a company from using the same purchasing strategy for every item.

Q12. Explain EOQ in Detail and Solve a Numerical Problem.

10 Marks 2023–2025 Core Paper

Marks: 10 PYQ priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Introduction

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) determines the order quantity that minimises the combined ordering cost and inventory holding cost, assuming relatively stable demand and other classical EOQ assumptions.

Diagram

ANNUAL COST
   │
   │\        Holding Cost
   │ \          /
   │  \        /
   │   \      /
   │    \    /
   │     \  /
   │      \/  ← Minimum Total Cost
   │      /
   │     /  
   │    /    
   │   /      
   └────────────────────→ ORDER QUANTITY
             Q*

Formula

$$ EOQ = Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}} $$

Where: - (D) = annual demand - (S) = ordering/setup cost per order - (H) = annual holding cost per unit

Total relevant annual cost:

$$ TC = \frac{D}{Q}S + \frac{Q}{2}H $$

Key Points

At EOQ: - Ordering cost decreases as order quantity increases. - Holding cost increases as order quantity increases. - EOQ is the point at which total relevant cost is minimum. - Average cycle inventory = (Q/2), assuming no safety stock. - At the mathematical EOQ, annual ordering cost approximately equals annual holding cost.

Classical Assumptions

  • Demand is known and relatively constant.
  • Lead time is known.
  • Replenishment is instantaneous.
  • No stockouts.
  • No quantity discounts.
  • Holding and ordering costs are known.

Numerical Problem

Given: - Annual demand (D = 12,000) units - Ordering cost (S = \₹50) per order - Holding cost (H = \₹4) per unit/year - Lead time (L = 5) working days - Working days = 300/year

Step 1 --- Calculate EOQ

$$ Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}} $$

$$ = \sqrt{\frac{2(12,000)(50)}{4}} $$

$$ = \sqrt{300,000} $$

$$ Q^* = 547.72 $$

$$ \boxed{EOQ \approx 548\ units} $$

Step 2 --- Orders per Year

$$ N = \frac{D}{Q} $$

$$ N = \frac{12,000}{548} $$

$$ N \approx 21.90 $$

$$ \boxed{N \approx 22\ orders/year} $$

Step 3 --- Annual Ordering Cost

$$ OC = \frac{D}{Q}S $$

$$ = \frac{12,000}{548}(50) $$

$$ \approx \₹1,094.89 $$

Step 4 --- Annual Holding Cost

$$ HC = \frac{Q}{2}H $$

$$ = \frac{548}{2}(4) $$

$$ = \₹1,096 $$

Step 5 --- Total Relevant Annual Cost

$$ TC = OC + HC $$

$$ TC = 1,094.89 + 1,096 $$

$$ \boxed{TC \approx \₹2,190.89/year} $$

Step 6 --- Cycle Time

$$ T = \frac{Q}{D}\times300 $$

$$ = \frac{548}{12,000}\times300 $$

$$ \boxed{T \approx 13.7\ working\ days} $$

Step 7 --- Reorder Point

Daily demand:

$$ d = \frac{12,000}{300}=40 units/day $$

Deterministic ROP:

$$ ROP=dL $$

$$ =40(5) $$

$$ \boxed{ROP=200\ units} $$

Interpretation

The firm should order approximately 548 units whenever inventory reaches 200 units. The 200-unit ROP covers expected demand during the five-day lead time.

Example

A spare-parts distributor with stable annual demand can use EOQ to balance frequent small orders against the cost of carrying larger inventories.

Conclusion

EOQ is a fundamental inventory decision model because it converts the ordering-versus-holding-cost trade-off into a quantitative order policy.

Q13. Explain Safety Stock and Reorder Point under Demand and Lead-Time Uncertainty.

10 Marks 2023–2025 Core Paper

Marks: 10 PYQ priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Introduction

Safety stock is additional inventory maintained to protect against uncertainty in demand and/or replenishment lead time. Reorder Point (ROP) determines when a replenishment order should be placed.

Diagram

INVENTORY
   │
   │
   │ 
SS │  \      ← Safety Stock
   │   
   │    
ROP│-----\--- Order Trigger
   │      
   │       
   │        
   │         
   └────────────────────────→ TIME
            ↑
       Replenishment arrives

Core Formulas

Demand variable, lead time constant

$$ SS=z\sigma_d\sqrt{L} $$

Lead time variable, demand constant

$$ SS=z d\sigma_L $$

Both demand and lead time variable

$$ SS=z\sqrt{L\sigma_d^2+d^2\sigma_L^2} $$

Reorder Point

$$ ROP=dL+SS $$

Where: - (z) = service-level factor - (d) = average daily demand - (L) = average lead time - (\sigma_d) = standard deviation of daily demand - (\sigma_L) = standard deviation of lead time

Numerical Problem

A distributor has: - Mean daily demand (d=120) units - Daily demand SD (\sigma_d=30) - Mean lead time (L=16) days - Lead-time SD (\sigma_L=3) days - Target service level = 95% - (z=1.645)

Step 1 --- Expected Lead-Time Demand

$$ DL=dL $$

$$ =120(16) $$

$$ \boxed{DL=1,920\ units} $$

Step 2 --- Combined Standard Deviation

$$ \sigma_{DL} = \sqrt{L\sigma_d^2+d^2\sigma_L^2} $$

$$ \sqrt{16(30^2)+120^2(3^2)} $$

$$ \sqrt{16(900)+14,400(9)} $$

$$ \sqrt{14,400+129,600} $$

$$ \sqrt{144,000} $$

$$ \boxed{\sigma_{DL}\approx379.47} $$

Step 3 --- Safety Stock

$$ SS=z\sigma_{DL} $$

$$ =1.645(379.47) $$

$$ =624.23 $$

$$ \boxed{SS\approx625\ units} $$

Step 4 --- Reorder Point

$$ ROP=DL+SS $$

$$ =1,920+625 $$

$$ \boxed{ROP=2,545\ units} $$

Interpretation

When inventory position falls to approximately 2,545 units, the firm should trigger replenishment. The 625 units above expected lead-time demand provide protection against uncertainty.

Example

An electronics distributor facing volatile demand and supplier lead times can increase safety stock when the required service level increases.

Conclusion

Safety stock converts uncertainty into a measurable inventory buffer, while ROP converts the buffer into an operational replenishment trigger.

Q14. Explain the Single-Period / Newsvendor Model and Solve a Numerical.

10 Marks 2023–2025 Core Paper

Marks: 10 PYQ priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Introduction

The single-period or Newsvendor model is used when inventory has a limited selling period and the decision-maker must balance the cost of ordering too little against ordering too much.

Typical examples include: - Fashion products. - Fresh food. - Seasonal goods. - Newspapers. - Event merchandise.

Diagram

             SINGLE-PERIOD DECISION
                       │
          ┌────────────┴────────────┐
          ↓                         ↓
     ORDER TOO LITTLE          ORDER TOO MUCH
          │                         │
     UNDERSTOCK                 OVERSTOCK
          │                         │
   Lost contribution          Unsold inventory
       margin                  Salvage loss
          │                         │
          └────────────┬────────────┘
                       ↓
               OPTIMAL QUANTITY

Formula

Understocking cost:

$$ C_u=P-C $$

Overstocking cost:

$$ C_o=C-S $$

Critical fractile:

$$ CR=\frac{C_u}{C_u+C_o} $$

For normally distributed demand:

$$ Q^*=\mu+z\sigma $$

where:

$$ \Phi(z)=CR $$

Numerical Problem

Given: - Selling price (P=\₹400) - Purchase cost (C=\₹150) - Salvage value (S=\₹50) - Mean demand (\mu=1,200) - Demand SD (\sigma=250)

Step 1 --- Understock Cost

$$ C_u=P-C $$

$$ =400-150 $$

$$ \boxed{C_u=\₹250} $$

Step 2 --- Overstock Cost

$$ C_o=C-S $$

$$ =150-50 $$

$$ \boxed{C_o=\₹100} $$

Step 3 --- Critical Fractile

$$ CR=\frac{250}{250+100} $$

$$ =\frac{250}{350} $$

$$ \boxed{CR=0.7143} $$

Thus the target service probability is 71.43%.

Step 4 --- Find (z)

From the standard normal table:

$$ \Phi(z)=0.7143 $$

$$ z\approx0.566 $$

Step 5 --- Calculate Optimal Quantity

$$ Q^*=\mu+z\sigma $$

$$ =1,200+(0.566)(250) $$

$$ =1,200+141.5 $$

$$ \boxed{Q^*\approx1,342\ coats} $$

Step 6 --- Stockout Probability

$$ P(\text{stockout})=1-CR $$

$$ =1-0.7143 $$

$$ \boxed{P(\text{stockout})=28.57\%} $$

Interpretation

Because the profit lost from understocking is much larger than the loss from overstocking, the firm should stock more than the mean demand.

Example

A fashion retailer selling a winter collection cannot keep the same inventory decision after the season because the remaining products may need to be heavily discounted.

Conclusion

The Newsvendor model gives the economically optimal service level and stocking quantity when demand is uncertain and the selling period is limited.

Introduction

Incoterms 2020, published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), are standardised trade terms that define important responsibilities for delivery, costs and risk between buyer and seller.

B. Sea / Inland Waterway Only

  • FAS
  • FOB

Key Concept: Cost ≠ Risk

This is a major examination trap.

Under C rules such as CPT, CIP, CFR and CIF, the seller may pay the main carriage, but risk can transfer much earlier.

Comparison

Term Main Meaning Risk Transfer

EXW Seller makes goods Seller's premises available

FCA Seller delivers to Named delivery point carrier/named point

CPT Seller pays carriage When goods handed to carrier

CIP CPT + seller-arranged When goods handed to insurance carrier

DAP Delivered at Destination before destination, ready for unloading unloading

FOB Free on Board When goods are on board vessel

CFR Cost and Freight When goods are on board vessel

CIF Cost, Insurance and When goods are on board Freight vessel

FOB vs CIF

FOB

  • Sea/inland waterway.
  • Seller loads goods on board.
  • Buyer normally arranges main ocean freight and insurance.
  • Risk transfers once goods are on board.

CIF

  • Sea/inland waterway.
  • Seller pays ocean freight to destination port.
  • Seller arranges the required insurance.
  • Risk still transfers when goods are on board at origin.

Therefore:

CIF does not mean seller bears transit risk until destination.

FCA and Containers

For containerised and multimodal shipments, FCA is generally more appropriate than FOB, depending on the contractual arrangement, because FOB is designed for sea/inland-waterway delivery where goods are loaded directly on board the vessel.

DDP

DDP places the greatest overall obligation on the seller because the seller is responsible for delivery with import clearance and applicable import duties/taxes under the term.

Example

An Indian exporter shipping a container through a multimodal transport chain may prefer FCA rather than FOB. A bulk commodity loaded directly onto a vessel may be appropriately contracted under FOB.

Conclusion

The key to answering Incoterms questions is to distinguish:

Who pays? → Who performs? → Where does risk transfer? → What transport mode applies?

$$ =\frac{Total\ Logistics\ Cost}{Gross\ Revenue}\times100 $$

Example

A firm should not select a transport option only because its freight charge is low if it creates higher inventory, warehousing or stockout costs.

Conclusion

Logistics decisions should minimise total system cost, not an isolated cost component.

Q24. Explain SCOR / Supply Chain Drivers and Strategic Fit.

6 Marks 2024 Exam Paper

Marks: 6

Introduction

A supply chain's performance depends on how its structural and operational drivers are configured to support the firm's competitive strategy.

Six Drivers

             SUPPLY CHAIN DRIVERS
                    │
 ┌──────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┬───────┐
 ↓      ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓
Facilities Inventory Transport Information Sourcing Pricing

Key Points

Facilities

Location, capacity and centralisation.

Inventory

Cycle stock, safety stock and working capital.

Transportation

Mode, route, speed and consolidation.

Information

Visibility, forecasting and coordination.

Sourcing

Supplier selection, outsourcing and partnerships.

Pricing

Pricing decisions influence demand patterns and order behaviour.

Strategic Fit

Low uncertainty + predictable demand
             ↓
        EFFICIENCY

High uncertainty + innovative products
             ↓
        RESPONSIVENESS

Example

A commodity FMCG product can prioritise scale and low logistics cost, while a short-life fashion product may prioritise flexibility and speed.

Conclusion

Supply-chain drivers should be configured as an integrated system rather than optimised independently.

Critical Exam Traps

Trap 1

CIF ≠ seller bears risk until destination.

Risk transfers when goods are on board at origin, although seller pays freight and arranges insurance.

Trap 2

FOB/CIF are not general multimodal terms.

They are for sea/inland waterway transport.

Trap 3

Incoterms do not determine ownership/title.

Trap 4

Cost responsibility and risk responsibility are different.

Final Examination Hall Strategy & Scoring Blueprint

For a 5-Mark Question

Definition → Diagram → 4 key points → Example → Conclusion

For a 6-Mark Question

Definition → Diagram/Matrix → Detailed comparison → Example → Conclusion

For a 10-Mark Question

Definition → Large diagram → Detailed framework → Formula/application/case → Managerial interpretation → Conclusion

For a Numerical

Always write:

GIVEN
  ↓
FORMULA
  ↓
SUBSTITUTION
  ↓
CALCULATION
  ↓
FINAL ANSWER
  ↓
INTERPRETATION

Golden Rule

Do not stop at the numerical answer. Explain what the number means operationally.

For example:

"Therefore, the EOQ is 548 units. The firm should order approximately 548 units per cycle; when inventory position reaches the ROP of 200 units, replenishment should be triggered."

That final sentence converts a calculation into a management answer.

One-Line Memory Hooks

  • EOQ: Balance ordering cost and holding cost.
  • ROP: When to order.
  • Safety Stock: Protection against uncertainty.
  • Newsvendor: How much to stock for one selling period.
  • S&OP: One agreed demand-supply plan.
  • S&OE: Short-term execution control.
  • CPFR: Collaborate using shared information.
  • Kraljic: Match sourcing strategy to profit impact and supply risk.
  • SCOR: Plan → Source → Make → Deliver → Return.
  • Incoterms: Know mode + cost + risk transfer.
  • Smart Warehouse: Sense → Decide → Act → Learn.
  • Network Design: Balance cost, service, inventory and resilience.
  • ESG: Environmental + Social + Governance.
  • Reverse Logistics: Move products/value backward.
  • Resilience: Prepare → Absorb → Respond → Recover → Learn.
