What is Entrepreneurship & Venture Capital?

Entrepreneurship is the pursuit of opportunity beyond resources currently controlled (Howard Stevenson, Harvard Business School). It is fundamentally distinct from standard corporate management because it operates under conditions of extreme ambiguity, non-linear risk, and asymmetric upside.

Venture Capital (VC) is the specialized financial intermediary model that fuels high-growth entrepreneurship. VCs pool capital from limited partners (endowments, pension funds, family offices) to take non-controlling, high-risk equity stakes in early-stage ventures that possess the potential to achieve 10x to 100x exits via IPO or acquisition.

The entrepreneurial process encompasses: (1) Opportunity Recognition, (2) Business Model Formulation, (3) Resource Mobilization & Seed Funding, (4) Product-Market Fit Iteration, and (5) Hyper-Scaling & Liquidity.

SECTION 02 OF 10

Why Entrepreneurship & VC Matter in an MBA

Even for MBA graduates who do not intend to launch a startup immediately, entrepreneurial fluency is essential across corporate and investment roles:

  • Corporate Innovation & Intrapreneurship: Established enterprises face the "innovator's dilemma." Large firms increasingly deploy venture studios, corporate venture capital (CVC), and agile startup methodologies to combat disruption.
  • Private Equity & Venture Investing: Understanding how to evaluate founder teams, analyze unit economics, and negotiate investor rights (liquidation preferences, anti-dilution, board control) is central to private markets careers.
  • General Management: Entrepreneurship instills relentless resourcefulness, rapid hypothesis testing, and decisive action with incomplete data.

Core Concepts of Startup Creation & VC

1. Product-Market Fit (PMF)

Marc Andreessen’s definition: being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market. Evidenced by strong organic pull, high customer retention curves, and customers actively referring others.

2. The Lean Startup Methodology (Ries/Blank)

Replacing comprehensive upfront business plans with rapid Build-Measure-Learn feedback loops. Developing a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) to test falsifiable commercial hypotheses and preserve cash runway.

3. The Power Law of Venture Returns

Venture returns follow a Pareto distribution rather than a normal Gaussian bell curve. In a top VC fund, 1 or 2 outlier investments ("fund returners") generate more financial return than the remaining 95% of portfolio companies combined.

4. Dilution & Cap Table Dynamics

Each funding round issues new shares to incoming investors and employee option pools, diluting existing founders and early backers while aiming to grow the total pie value exponentially.

SECTION 05 OF 10

Major Frameworks and Analytical Models

1. The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder)

A one-page strategic blueprint covering 9 essential building blocks: Value Propositions, Customer Segments, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships, and Cost Structure.

2. Venture Funding Lifecycle

Pre-Seed / Angel: Concept validation & prototype ($100k-$500k). Seed Round: MVP & initial customer traction ($1M-$3M). Series A: Validated Product-Market Fit & scalable distribution ($5M-$15M). Series B & C: Hyper-scaling, international expansion, and market dominance ($20M-$100M+). Exit: IPO or M&A.

3. Sahlman’s Entrepreneurial Framework (HBS)

Analyzes new ventures through the dynamic fit between 4 interdependent variables: The People (capabilities, ethics), The Opportunity (industry structure, economics), The Context (macroeconomic & regulatory tailwinds), and The Deal (risk-reward contracts between founders and capital providers).

Important Formulas & Quantitative Measures

Pre-Money, Post-Money & Investor Ownership Post-Money = Pre-Money + Investment  |  Investor Ownership % = Investment / Post-Money
Example: $2M invested at $8M Pre-Money yields a $10M Post-Money valuation; the investor owns $2M / $10M = 20% of the firm.
Venture Capital Method for Valuation Exit Valuation = Projected Terminal Revenue × Industry Multiple  |  Post-Money = Exit Valuation / (1 + Target IRR)^t
Discounted back using steep early-stage hurdle rates (target IRR of 40%–60% to account for high portfolio mortality rates).
Net Burn Rate & Runway Runway (Months) = Cash Balance / [Operating Expenses - Cash Receipts]
The foundational solvency metric for cash-flow negative ventures.

Typical Strategic Business Decisions Involved

Bootstrapping vs. Venture Capital

Choosing between retaining 100% equity ownership with steady profitability versus taking venture dilution to achieve winner-take-most market leadership before competitors arrive.

Equity Allocation & Vesting Schedules

Structuring 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff for founder equity to protect the company if a co-founder departs prematurely, preventing "dead equity" on the cap table.

Pivot or Persevere

Interpreting customer signal vs. noise: when customer acquisition costs remain prohibitively high, deciding whether to change the target segment, revise the value proposition, or alter the business model.

Board Composition & Founder Control

Managing board seats across preferred investors, founders, and independent industry directors. Avoiding loss of founder voting control before Series B.

SECTION 08 OF 10

Real-World Business Applications

Case 1: Airbnb’s Pivot to Product-Market Fit

Originally "AirBed & Breakfast," the founders struggled for two years with negligible revenue until they visited New York hosts in person, upgraded low-quality user photos to professional photography, and solved customer trust through two-sided peer reviews and host insurance guarantees.

Case 2: Term Sheet Down-Rounds & Anti-Dilution Triggers

In tech downturns (e.g., 2022-2023), companies that raised capital at inflated valuations face "down-rounds." Full-ratchet or broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution clauses re-price existing investor stakes, causing massive dilution to common equity and employee options.

SECTION 09 OF 10

Cross-Disciplinary Connections to Other MBA Fields

  • Corporate Finance: Venture capital requires understanding option pricing models, terminal value estimation, and waterfall distribution models under complex preferred stock covenants.
  • Organizational Behaviour: Early-stage organizational culture, founding team dynamics, and compensation equity structuring determine startup survival.
  • Strategy: Startups must exploit asymmetric advantages (speed, regulatory gray areas, unbundled value chains) where incumbents cannot respond without harming their existing profit centers.
  • Law & Governance: Drafting shareholder agreements, intellectual property assignment contracts, and navigating Delaware corporate law are daily entrepreneurial requirements.
