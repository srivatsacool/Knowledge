What is Corporate Finance & Valuation?

Corporate Finance is the discipline dedicated to answering three fundamental managerial questions:

  1. The Investment Decision (Capital Budgeting): What long-term projects, technologies, and acquisitions should the firm invest in to generate positive economic value?
  2. The Financing Decision (Capital Structure): How should the firm raise capital to fund these investments—through debt, equity, or hybrid securities?
  3. The Liquidity / Dividend Decision (Working Capital & Payouts): How much cash should be retained inside the business for growth versus returned to shareholders via dividends or share repurchases?

The Governing Principle of Corporate Finance: An enterprise creates shareholder value if and only if it generates a return on invested capital (ROIC) greater than its opportunity cost of capital (WACC). Any project that yields less than WACC destroys value, regardless of its accounting profit.

SECTION 02 OF 10

Why Corporate Finance Matters in an MBA

Corporate finance provides the universal quantitative metric—Net Present Value (NPV)—that allows general managers to compare fundamentally different strategic choices across industries, time horizons, and risk profiles.

Whether evaluating an enterprise software implementation, an offshore manufacturing plant, or a multi-billion dollar M&A tender offer, an MBA executive must be able to:

  • Determine the correct, risk-adjusted discount rate for any business initiative.
  • Construct robust Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) models that isolate cash from accounting distortions.
  • Understand how financial leverage amplifies both equity returns and bankruptcy risk.
SECTION 03 OF 10

Core Concepts of Corporate Finance & Valuation

1. Time Value of Money (TVM)

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow due to its earning capacity and inflation. Future cash flows must be discounted to present value using an appropriate opportunity cost of capital.

2. Net Present Value (NPV) Rule

The gold standard of capital budgeting: Accept all independent projects with NPV > 0. Reject projects with NPV < 0. For mutually exclusive projects, select the one with the highest positive NPV.

3. Risk-Return Tradeoff & CAPM

Investors demand higher expected returns for bearing non-diversifiable systematic risk (Market Beta). Diversifiable (idiosyncratic) risk is eliminated through broad portfolio diversification and earns no risk premium.

4. Modigliani-Miller Theorems (M&M)

In frictionless markets (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs), firm value is independent of capital structure. In the presence of corporate taxes, debt creates value through the interest tax shield, offset by costs of financial distress.

Key Terminology Every Manager Must Know

TermDefinitionCorporate Finance Application
WACCWeighted Average Cost of Capital: the blended hurdle rate reflecting the required return on equity and after-tax cost of debt.The discount rate used in unlevered DCF valuation models to discount Free Cash Flows to the Firm (FCFF).
Beta (β)A measure of the sensitivity of a stock’s returns relative to the overall market portfolio.β = 1 implies equal volatility with market; β > 1 implies cyclicality/high sensitivity; β < 1 indicates defensive business models.
Terminal Value (TV)The present value of all future cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period (typically year 5 or 10).Often accounts for 60% to 85% of total enterprise value in a DCF model. Calculated via Gordon Growth Model or Exit Multiples.
Internal Rate of Return (IRR)The discount rate that equates the NPV of all future cash flows from an investment to zero.Popular metric in private equity; however, IRR has flaws (reinvestment rate assumption, multiple IRRs with non-normal cash flows).
Enterprise Value (EV)Total economic value of the operating business: EV = Market Cap + Total Debt - Cash.Represents the acquisition price required to buy the entire business free of debt. Used in multiples like EV/EBITDA.
SECTION 05 OF 10

Major Frameworks and Analytical Models

1. The Three Primary Valuation Methodologies

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): Intrinsic valuation based on projected future unlevered free cash flows discounted at WACC plus terminal value.
  • Comparable Company Analysis (Public Comps): Relative valuation using market multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Revenue) of publicly traded peers.
  • Precedent Transactions (M&A Comps): Multiples paid in historical acquisitions of similar companies, incorporating control premiums.

2. Trade-Off Theory vs. Pecking Order Theory

Trade-Off Theory: Optimal capital structure balances the tax advantages of debt (tax shield) against the costs of financial distress and bankruptcy.

Pecking Order Theory (Myers & Majluf): Due to information asymmetry, managers prefer internal cash first, debt second, and equity issuance as a last resort.

SECTION 06 OF 10

Important Formulas & Quantitative Measures

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) WACC = (E / V) × r_e + (D / V) × r_d × (1 - t)
Where E is market value of equity, D is market value of debt, V = E + D, r_e is cost of equity, r_d is pre-tax cost of debt, and t is the marginal corporate tax rate.
Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) - Cost of Equity r_e = r_f + β × (r_m - r_f) = r_f + β × ERP
Where r_f is the risk-free rate (e.g., 10-year US Treasury yield), β is the asset beta, and ERP is the Equity Risk Premium (historically 4.5%–6.0%).
Gordon Growth Terminal Value Terminal Value_n = [FCFF_(n+1)] / [WACC - g] = [FCFF_n × (1 + g)] / [WACC - g]
Where g is the perpetual sustainable growth rate, which theoretically must not exceed the long-term GDP growth rate (typically 2.0%–3.0%).
SECTION 07 OF 10

Typical Strategic Business Decisions Involved

Capital Budgeting Selection

Choosing between competing multi-million dollar capital expenditure proposals using NPV, IRR, and payback period under capital rationing constraints.

Optimal Capital Structure Targeting

Determining the target debt-to-equity ratio: raising low-cost investment-grade bond debt to boost ROE while protecting credit ratings (maintaining BBB/A ratings).

Dividends vs. Share Buybacks

Returning surplus cash to investors: share repurchases offer tax advantages and signaling benefits when stock is undervalued; regular dividends appeal to institutional income investors.

M&A Accretion / Dilution Analysis

Modeling whether an acquisition will increase or decrease pro-forma Earnings Per Share (EPS), evaluating required post-merger cost and revenue synergies.

SECTION 08 OF 10

Real-World Business Applications

Case 1: Apple’s Massive Capital Return Program

Under Tim Cook, Apple shifted from hoarding over $200 billion in overseas cash to issuing low-interest corporate debt to fund hundreds of billions in share repurchases, retiring over 35% of its outstanding shares and dramatically boosting EPS and ROIC.

Case 2: The Leveraged Buyout (LBO) Model

Private equity firms (e.g., KKR, Blackstone) use 60%–80% debt to acquire cash-flow generative companies, using target company cash flows to pay down the debt over 5 years. This massive financial leverage amplifies equity returns, turning modest business growth into 25%+ fund IRRs.

SECTION 09 OF 10

Cross-Disciplinary Connections to Other MBA Fields

  • Strategy: Corporate strategy dictates where competitive moats exist; finance calculates the monetary value of those moats through DCF cash flows and terminal value premiums.
  • Operations: Supply chain optimizations and inventory reductions directly release cash flow, lowering working capital requirements and boosting enterprise valuation.
  • Law & Corporate Governance: Fiduciary duties of boards of directors during takeover defenses (Revlon duties, poison pills, proxy contests) are grounded in shareholder value maximization.

What a Student Should Learn Next

  • Mergers, Acquisitions & Restructuring: Structuring asset purchases vs. stock sales, tax-free reorganizations, and distressed debt turnaround.
